## 1. Overview: From Cells to Circuits to Systems

The nervous system is organized hierarchically:

```
Individual Cells → Circuits → Systems → Behaviour / Cognition
```

This lecture focuses on the **individual cell level** — understanding how single neurons and glial cells work is the essential foundation before studying circuits and systems.

> 💡 **Additional Context (from assistant):** Think of neurons as the "processors" of a biological computer. Before understanding how a computer program runs, you need to understand how a single transistor works. Neurons are the transistors of the brain.

---

## 2. Two Classes of Nervous System Cells

The nervous system contains **two fundamental classes** of cells:

| Property | **Neurons** | **Glial Cells (Neuroglia)** |
|---|---|---|
| Primary function | Signaling — receive, process, transmit information | Support neurons structurally, immunologically, nutritionally |
| Relative number | ~100 billion | 2–10× more than neurons |
| Signal generation | Yes (electrical & chemical) | Limited (mainly modulatory) |
| Key examples | Motor neurons, sensory neurons, interneurons | Microglia, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, Schwann cells |

> ⚠️ **Exam Tip:** You are expected to **distinguish neurons from glial cells** and describe the roles of each. The ratio (2–10× more glia than neurons) is a commonly tested fact.

## 3. Glial Cells (Neuroglia)

**Neuroglia** literally means "nerve glue." Glial cells greatly outnumber neurons (2–10× more in the vertebrate CNS) and provide four key support functions:

- **Structural** — physically scaffold the nervous system
- **Immune** — respond to injury and infection
- **Nourishment** — deliver metabolites, regulate the chemical environment
- **Signaling** — modulate synaptic transmission and conduct signals efficiently

### 3.1 Microglia — Immune Support

![Microglia morphology and phagocytic activity](https://images/slide_10_figure_01_microglia.png)
> 📌 **Note:** This image is located on **Slides 10–12** of the provided slides (figure title: "Immune support microglia"). Please place the file as `images/slide_10_figure_01_microglia.png` in the same GitHub repository — it will load automatically once added.

**Microglia** are the **immune cells of the brain**. They act as the brain's first and primary form of active immune defense.

Key functions:
- **Surveillance**: Continuously scan the CNS for signs of damage, infection, or dead cells
- **Phagocytosis**: Engulf and destroy pathogens, dead cells, and toxic debris
- **Antigen presentation**: Become mobilized as phagocytes during injury, infection, or neurodegeneration
- **Inflammatory response**: Initiate cellular responses to remove toxic agents

**Clinical Link — Alzheimer's Disease:**
In neurodegenerative disorders such as **Alzheimer's disease**, microglia may become **hyperactivated**, promoting chronic neuroinflammation. This can contribute to the formation of characteristic toxic protein deposits:
- **Amyloid plaques** (extracellular deposits of β-amyloid protein)
- **Neurofibrillary tangles** (intracellular aggregates of tau protein)

> ⚠️ **Exam Tip:** Know the link between microglial hyperactivation and Alzheimer's pathology. This bridges cell biology with clinical neuroscience.

### 3.2 Astrocytes — Nourishing Support

![Astrocyte star-shaped morphology and relationship to vasculature](https://images/slide_13_figure_02_astrocytes.png)
> 📌 **Note:** This image is located on **Slides 13–14** of the provided slides (figure title: "Nourishing support astrocytes"). Please place the file as `images/slide_13_figure_02_astrocytes.png` in the same GitHub repository — it will load automatically once added.

**Astrocytes** are star-shaped glial cells that constitute **nearly half** of all brain cells. They:

- **Surround neurons** and are in close contact with the brain's vasculature (blood vessels)
- **Nourish neurons** by transferring metabolites from blood vessels to neurons
- **Regulate ion and neurotransmitter concentrations** in the extracellular space (e.g., clearing excess K⁺ or glutamate after neuronal activity)
- **Communicate with neurons** to modulate synaptic signaling (bidirectional)
- **Maintain the Blood-Brain Barrier (BBB)** — the selective barrier between CNS tissue and blood, which protects the brain from harmful substances in the bloodstream

> 💡 **Additional Context (from assistant):** The Blood-Brain Barrier is why many drugs cannot treat brain diseases effectively — they cannot cross the BBB. Astrocyte end-feet (extensions) wrap around capillaries and are critical to maintaining this barrier. Disruption of astrocyte function (e.g., in stroke or brain trauma) compromises the BBB and leads to brain edema.

### 3.3 Oligodendrocytes & Schwann Cells — Signaling Support

![Oligodendrocyte myelinating multiple axons in the CNS](https://images/slide_15_figure_03_oligodendrocytes.png)
> 📌 **Note:** This image is located on **Slides 15–17** of the provided slides (figure title: "Signaling support — oligodendrocytes and Schwann cells"). Please place the file as `images/slide_15_figure_03_oligodendrocytes.png` in the same GitHub repository — it will load automatically once added.

| Property | **Oligodendrocytes** | **Schwann Cells** |
|---|---|---|
| Location | CNS (brain + spinal cord) | PNS (peripheral nerves) |
| Function | Produce myelin for CNS axons | Produce myelin for PNS axons |
| Coverage | One oligodendrocyte myelinates **multiple** axons | One Schwann cell myelinates **one** axon segment |

Both produce **myelin** — thin sheets of fatty insulating material that wrap concentrically around axons many times. Myelin:
- Insulates the axon (reduces voltage loss)
- Allows **rapid conduction** of electrical signals (saltatory conduction — see Section 11)
- Is **white** in color, giving **white matter** its name

**Clinical Link — Multiple Sclerosis (MS):**
In **Multiple Sclerosis**, the immune system mistakenly attacks **oligodendrocytes** (autoimmune disease). This damages and scars the myelin sheath in the CNS (demyelination), causing:
- Slowing or disruption of nerve signal transmission
- Symptoms including muscle weakness, visual problems, coordination difficulties

> ⚠️ **Exam Tip:** The distinction between oligodendrocytes (CNS) and Schwann cells (PNS) is frequently examined. Also know that MS affects oligodendrocytes (CNS), not Schwann cells.

## 4. Neurons — Structure & Function

There are approximately **100 billion neurons** in the nervous system, with **100 distinct types**. Despite their variety in shape and size (arborization patterns), they share a universal structural plan.

Neurons vary in **form, location, and interconnectivity**, and these variations are closely related to their functions. The term **arborization** refers to the number, branching, and disposition of a neuron's projections (dendrites), which determines its computational ability and capacity for interaction.

### General cell components (shared with all eukaryotic cells):
- **Cell membrane**: Separates intracellular from extracellular space
- **Cytoplasm**: Intracellular fluid with ions (K⁺, Na⁺, Cl⁻, Ca²⁺) and proteins
- **Extracellular fluid**: The "bath" surrounding neurons, with similar ion types
- **Cell body (soma)**: Metabolic center; contains nucleus (genes) and endoplasmic reticulum (protein synthesis)

### Unique neuronal components:

![Neuron structure showing soma, dendrites, axon, and synaptic terminals](https://images/slide_22_figure_04_neuron_structure.png)
> 📌 **Note:** This image is located on **Slides 22–27** of the provided slides (figure title: "The structure of neurons"). Please place the file as `images/slide_22_figure_04_neuron_structure.png` in the same GitHub repository — it will load automatically once added.

### 4.1 Dendrites

- **Multiple** processes extending from the soma
- Represent the **receiving zone** (input zone) of the neuron
- Receive inputs from other neurons via synapses
- Can take many varied and complex forms depending on neuron type and location
- Shape (arborization) determines computational ability

### 4.2 Axon

- **Single** process extending from the soma
- Represents the **transmitting zone** of the neuron
- Carries signals **from** the input zone (dendrites) **to** the output zone (synapses)
- Can convey electrical signals over distances ranging from **0.1 mm to 2 m**
- Originates at the **axon hillock** — the trigger zone where action potentials are initiated

### 4.3 Synapse

- **Multiple** processes at the end of the axon
- Represent the **output zone** of the neuron
- Specialized structures where two neurons come into close contact
- Allow chemical or electrical signals to pass from one cell to the next
- Enable communication between neurons

> ⚠️ **Exam Tip:** Know the three unique neuronal components (dendrites, axon, synapse), their zone designations (receiving, transmitting, output), and structural characteristics (multiple vs. single process).

## 5. The Four Signaling Regions of a Neuron

All neurons share the **same organizational principle** of signaling, regardless of cell size, shape, neurotransmitter type, or function. There are **4 regions** that generate **4 types of signals**:

![Four signaling regions of a neuron with corresponding signal types](https://images/slide_30_figure_05_four_regions.png)
> 📌 **Note:** This image is located on **Slides 30–41** of the provided slides (figure title: "4 regions that generate 4 types of signal"). Please place the file as `images/slide_30_figure_05_four_regions.png` in the same GitHub repository — it will load automatically once added.

| Region | Zone | Signal Type | Properties |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Input region** | Dendrites / receptor cells | **Postsynaptic Potential (PSP)** or receptor potential | **Graded** in amplitude and duration; proportional to stimulus |
| **B. Trigger zone** | Axon hillock | **Trigger signal** | Integrates all PSPs; fires AP if threshold (−55 mV) is exceeded |
| **C. Conductive region** | Axon | **Action Potential (AP)** | All-or-none; constant amplitude and duration |
| **D. Output region** | Axon terminals / synapses | **Synaptic signal** | Frequency of APs → amount of neurotransmitter released |

**Information flow within a neuron:**
```
Dendrites (input) → Cell body (integration) → Axon hillock (trigger) 
   → Axon (conduction) → Axon terminals (output) → Next neuron
```

**Key principle — Graded PSPs → Frequency Code:**
> The graded amplitude and duration of PSPs is **translated into a frequency code** in the APs generated at the trigger zone.

- A stronger/longer input stimulus → **more frequent APs** (higher firing rate)
- The amplitude of the AP remains **constant** (all-or-none)
- Stimulus intensity is encoded in **AP frequency**, NOT in AP amplitude

**Example from slides:**
- Stimulus 2 has the **same duration but greater amplitude** than stimulus 1 → **increase in frequency** of APs → more neurotransmitter released
- Stimulus 3 has the **same amplitude as stimulus 2 but lasts longer** → **increase in duration** of the AP train → more neurotransmitter released

> ⚠️ **Exam Tip:** This is a core concept. The conversion from graded PSP amplitude/duration to AP **frequency** is fundamental. Be able to explain this transformation clearly, including why it is necessary (APs are all-or-none).

## 6. Resting Membrane Potential

The **resting membrane potential (RMP)** is the electrical potential difference across the neuron's cell membrane when the neuron is at rest.

$$V_{rest} \approx -70 \text{ mV}$$

This means the **inside of the cell is ~70 mV more negative** than the outside.

### Why does the RMP exist?

It arises from the **asymmetric distribution of ions** across the cell membrane, driven by **electrochemical forces** (combined electrical and chemical gradients):

- **K⁺ (Potassium)**: Higher concentration *inside* the cell; tends to flow *out* (chemical gradient), making the inside more negative
- **Na⁺ (Sodium)**: Higher concentration *outside* the cell; tends to flow *in* (chemical gradient)
- **Cl⁻ (Chloride)**: Higher concentration *outside*
- The **Na⁺/K⁺ pump** actively maintains the ion asymmetry (3 Na⁺ out for every 2 K⁺ in)

> 💡 **Additional Context (from assistant):** Think of the resting membrane potential as a **compressed spring** or a **charged battery**. The energy stored in this separation of charges is available to "do work" — i.e., generate electrical signals. Without this stored energy, neurons could not fire.

### Functional significance:
- The RMP is the **baseline** on which all neuronal signaling occurs
- It can be **rapidly and significantly altered** by ion flow through channels, serving as the signaling mechanism
- Two types of potential changes:
  - **Depolarization**: Membrane potential becomes *less* negative (moves toward 0 mV and beyond) → *excitatory*
  - **Hyperpolarization**: Membrane potential becomes *more* negative → *inhibitory*

> ⚠️ **Exam Tip:** Know the RMP value (−70 mV), what maintains it (ion asymmetry + Na⁺/K⁺ pump), and the definitions of depolarization and hyperpolarization.

## 7. Postsynaptic Potentials (PSPs)

**Postsynaptic Potentials (PSPs)** are small, **graded** changes in membrane potential that occur at the dendrites/soma when neurotransmitters bind to postsynaptic receptors.

### Types:
- **EPSP (Excitatory Postsynaptic Potential)**: Depolarizes the membrane (makes it more positive); pushes the cell **toward** threshold
- **IPSP (Inhibitory Postsynaptic Potential)**: Hyperpolarizes the membrane (makes it more negative); pushes the cell **away from** threshold

### Key properties of PSPs:

| Property | Description |
|---|---|
| **Graded** | Amplitude is proportional to stimulus strength |
| **Small amplitude** | Too small alone to trigger an AP |
| **Passively conducted** | Spread passively through dendrites and cell body (no active regeneration) |
| **Decremental conduction** | Signal **diminishes with distance** from synapse (decays) |
| **Short range** | Maximum travel distance: ~**1 mm** |

> 💡 **Additional Context (from assistant):** PSPs are like small ripples in water. If you drop a stone (one EPSP) in a pond, the ripple is visible nearby but fades quickly. To make a big wave at a distant point (the axon hillock), you need many stones dropped simultaneously or in rapid succession — that's summation.

### Why PSPs alone can't trigger an AP:
- A **single EPSP** is NOT enough to trigger neuronal firing
- PSPs decay with distance (decremental) — they cannot travel the full length of an axon (which can be >1 m in humans)
- → **Summation is required** (see Section 8)

> ⚠️ **Exam Tip:** Be clear on the contrast between PSPs (graded, decremental, short range) and Action Potentials (all-or-none, non-decremental, long range).

## 8. Spatial & Temporal Summation

Because a single EPSP is insufficient to trigger an AP, neurons integrate multiple inputs at the **axon hillock**.

![Spatial and temporal summation at the axon hillock](https://images/slide_50_figure_06_summation.png)
> 📌 **Note:** This image is located on **Slide 50** of the provided slides (figure title: "Spatial and Temporal Summation"). Please place the file as `images/slide_50_figure_06_summation.png` in the same GitHub repository — it will load automatically once added.

### Spatial Summation
**Definition:** Summation of excitatory and inhibitory PSPs received at **spatially separate synapses** (different locations on the dendrites/soma) at the **same time**.

```
Dendrite 1 (EPSP) ──┐
                     ├──→ Axon Hillock → sums all → AP if ≥ −55 mV
Dendrite 2 (EPSP) ──┘
```

**Example:**
- Synapse A fires → EPSP of +5 mV
- Synapse B fires simultaneously → EPSP of +5 mV
- Combined effect at axon hillock: +10 mV → pushes closer to threshold

### Temporal Summation
**Definition:** Summation of excitatory and inhibitory PSPs received at the **same synapse** but at **different time points** (rapid successive firing before the previous PSP has decayed).

```
Time 1: EPSP → +5 mV (still active)
Time 2: EPSP → +5 mV added
Combined: +10 mV
```

> ⚠️ **Exam Tip:** Know both types of summation, their definitions, and that summation occurs at the **axon hillock** (trigger zone). Input from **many presynaptic neurons** is needed to generate an AP in most neurons.

> 💡 **Additional Context (from assistant):** EPSPs and IPSPs are summed **algebraically**. If an EPSP (+5 mV) and an IPSP (−5 mV) arrive simultaneously, they cancel out. This means inhibitory neurons can "veto" excitatory signals — critical for fine-tuned neural control.

### Mathematical Example:

**Given:** Resting potential = −70 mV. Threshold = −55 mV. Each EPSP = +3 mV.

**Question:** How many simultaneous EPSPs are needed to reach threshold (ignoring IPSPs)?

**Solution:**
$$\Delta V_{needed} = -55 - (-70) = +15 \text{ mV}$$
$$n = \frac{15}{3} = 5 \text{ EPSPs}$$

**Answer:** A minimum of **5 simultaneous EPSPs** of +3 mV each are needed.

---

**Additional Example 1:**
Each EPSP = +2 mV, each IPSP = −2 mV. RMP = −70 mV. Threshold = −55 mV.
If 10 EPSPs and 3 IPSPs arrive simultaneously, does the neuron fire?

$$\Delta V = (10 \times 2) + (3 \times -2) = 20 - 6 = +14 \text{ mV}$$
$$V_{membrane} = -70 + 14 = -56 \text{ mV}$$
$$-56 \text{ mV} > -55 \text{ mV? No. The neuron does NOT fire.}$$

**Answer:** The membrane reaches −56 mV, which is just below threshold (−55 mV), so **no AP is generated**.

---

**Additional Example 2:**
RMP = −70 mV. Threshold = −55 mV. Each EPSP = +4 mV. Each IPSP = −3 mV.
A neuron receives 6 EPSPs and 2 IPSPs. Does it fire?

$$\Delta V = (6 \times 4) + (2 \times -3) = 24 - 6 = +18 \text{ mV}$$
$$V_{membrane} = -70 + 18 = -52 \text{ mV}$$
$$-52 > -55? \text{ Yes.}$$

**Answer:** Membrane reaches −52 mV > threshold (−55 mV) → **AP is generated**.

---

**Additional Example 3:**
A neuron receives temporal summation from one synapse. Two EPSPs arrive 2 ms apart (before the first has fully decayed). First EPSP contributes +6 mV; second contributes +5 mV (slightly decayed first). RMP = −70 mV. Threshold = −55 mV.

$$V_{membrane} = -70 + 6 + 5 = -59 \text{ mV}$$
$$-59 > -55? \text{ No.}$$

**Answer:** −59 mV < threshold → **No AP**. A third temporally summed input would be needed.

## 9. Action Potentials — Travelling Far

An **Action Potential (AP)** is a rapid, self-regenerating electrical signal that travels along the axon. It is the solution to the evolutionary challenge of long-distance signal transmission.

> **Evolutionary Challenge:** The maximum distance a passive current (e.g., EPSP) can travel is ~1 mm. Yet the longest human motor neuron axon can exceed **1 meter** (from spinal cord to toes). Sensory neurons can have axons **>1.5 meters** (toes to posterior column of spinal cord).

APs have **four key properties** that enable efficient long-distance neuronal signaling:

### 9.1 Threshold for Initiation

The AP is triggered **only if** summation of PSPs depolarizes the membrane to at least **−55 mV** (the threshold).

$$V_{threshold} = -55 \text{ mV}$$

**Functional implication:** Only *meaningful* (sufficiently strong) information leads to an AP — the neuron acts as a filter.

> ⚠️ **Exam Tip:** Know the threshold value (−55 mV) and its functional implication as a filter for meaningful signals.

### 9.2 Conducted Without Decrement

Unlike PSPs, APs are **actively propagated** (self-regenerative) — they do not decay with distance.

**How it works — ionic basis of the AP:**

![Action potential ionic mechanism — voltage-gated Na+ and K+ channels](https://images/slide_56_figure_07_AP_ionic_basis.png)
> 📌 **Note:** This image is located on **Slides 56–65** of the provided slides (figure title: "Action potential ionic basis"). Please place the file as `images/slide_56_figure_07_AP_ionic_basis.png` in the same GitHub repository — it will load automatically once added.

**Step-by-step AP mechanism:**

1. **Depolarization to threshold (−55 mV):** Summation of PSPs depolarizes the membrane to −55 mV
2. **Na⁺ channel opening:** Voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open → Na⁺ rushes **into** the neuron (down its concentration gradient)
3. **Regenerative cycle (positive feedback):**
   - Influx of Na⁺ (positive charges) neutralizes the negative charge inside
   - This further depolarizes the cell → more voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open
   - Cycle continues until the **Na⁺ equilibrium potential** (~+60 mV) is reached
   - Na⁺ channels then become **inactivated**
4. **Repolarization:** Voltage-gated **K⁺ channels** open → K⁺ flows **out** of the neuron (down concentration gradient)
   - This restores the negative charge inside → repolarization
5. **Hyperpolarization (undershoot):** K⁺ channels close slowly → K⁺ continues to leave briefly → membrane goes slightly below RMP → reaches **K⁺ equilibrium potential** (more negative than RMP)
6. **Return to RMP:** K⁺ channels close → membrane potential returns to −70 mV

```
    +60 mV |     /\
            |    /  \
    -55 mV --|---/----\--------  ← Threshold
    -70 mV --|--/------\---/--  ← RMP
            |          \/
             Depol.   Repol. Hyperpol.
```

**Functional implication:** The amplitude of the AP remains **constant**, even when conducted over great distances.

> ⚠️ **Exam Tip:** Be able to explain the full ionic sequence of an AP: Na⁺ in (depolarization) → Na⁺ inactivated + K⁺ out (repolarization) → hyperpolarization → return to RMP.

### 9.3 Refractory Period

**Definition:** A brief period of time **following an AP** when it is **impossible (or very difficult) to elicit another AP**.

**Causes:**
- **Membrane hyperpolarization** (after K⁺ efflux)
- **Inactivation of voltage-gated Na⁺ channels** (they cannot reopen immediately)

**Two phases:**
- **Absolute refractory period:** No AP can be generated at all (Na⁺ channels inactivated)
- **Relative refractory period:** AP can be generated but requires a **stronger-than-normal** stimulus (membrane is hyperpolarized)

**Functional implications:**
1. **Limits the maximum firing frequency** of a neuron → prevents overstimulation
2. **Ensures unidirectional flow** of APs: AP travels from axon hillock → axon terminal (cannot travel backward), because the region that just fired is in refractory state

> ⚠️ **Exam Tip:** Two functional implications of the refractory period are commonly examined: frequency limiting AND unidirectional conduction.

### 9.4 All-or-None Binary Nature

APs always have **similar amplitude and duration**, regardless of the size of the PSP that generated them.

- An AP evoked by a **large PSP** = same size as an AP evoked by a PSP **just above threshold**
- APs are **binary signals** (either fire or don't — like a digital 1 or 0)

**Functional implication:** The amplitude of the AP communicates **nothing** about the strength or duration of the input stimulus. → Information is encoded in **frequency** (see Section 10).

## 10. Frequency Coding — Communicating Stimulus Strength

Since APs are all-or-none, stimulus strength cannot be encoded in AP amplitude. Instead:

> **The graded amplitude and duration of PSPs is translated into a FREQUENCY CODE in the APs generated at the trigger zone.**

![Frequency coding: stimulus strength encoded as AP firing rate](https://images/slide_72_figure_08_frequency_coding.png)
> 📌 **Note:** This image is located on **Slides 38–40 and 71–73** of the provided slides (figure title: "Frequency coding"). Please place the file as `images/slide_72_figure_08_frequency_coding.png` in the same GitHub repository — it will load automatically once added.

| Stimulus change | Effect on APs | Effect on output |
|---|---|---|
| Stronger stimulus (greater amplitude) | Higher AP **frequency** | More neurotransmitter released |
| Longer stimulus (greater duration) | Longer **train** of APs | More neurotransmitter released over time |

> 💡 **Additional Context (from assistant):** This is analogous to **Pulse-Width Modulation (PWM)** in electronics — used to control motor speed or LED brightness with digital signals. A computer can't send "analog" strength values; instead it sends rapid on/off pulses at varying frequencies to simulate analog intensity. The brain uses the same principle.

**More intense stimuli** → **higher rates of action potentials**

At **chemical synapses**: frequency of APs → exactly how much neurotransmitter is released
At **electrical synapses**: the signal is directly transmitted to the postsynaptic neuron

## 11. Saltatory Conduction — Travelling Fast

Even with the AP's non-decremental nature, long axons need **fast** conduction. The solution: **myelination** and **saltatory conduction**.

![Saltatory conduction showing AP jumping between Nodes of Ranvier](https://images/slide_73_figure_09_saltatory_conduction.png)
> 📌 **Note:** This image is located on **Slide 73** of the provided slides (figure title: "Saltatory conduction"). Please place the file as `images/slide_73_figure_09_saltatory_conduction.png` in the same GitHub repository — it will load automatically once added.

**How it works:**
- Oligodendrocytes (CNS) / Schwann cells (PNS) wrap myelin around axons
- Myelin provides **insulation** → reduces voltage loss between nodes
- **Ion channels and pumps** are concentrated only at **Nodes of Ranvier** (gaps in the myelin sheath)
- APs can **only be generated at Nodes of Ranvier**
- The AP appears to **"jump"** from node to node (Latin: *saltare* = to jump)

**Advantages of saltatory conduction:**
1. **Speed**: Much faster than conduction in unmyelinated axons
2. **Energy efficiency**: Ion pumps only need to restore ion gradients at the nodes (not along the entire axon)

| Axon type | Conduction speed |
|---|---|
| Myelinated (large diameter) | Up to **120 m/s** |
| Unmyelinated (small diameter) | ~0.5–2 m/s |

> ⚠️ **Exam Tip:** Know the roles of myelin, Nodes of Ranvier, and the two advantages of saltatory conduction (speed + energy efficiency). Link to MS (loss of myelin → disrupted saltatory conduction).

## 12. Principle of Dynamic Polarization

The **Principle of Dynamic Polarization** (formulated by **Santiago Ramón y Cajal**, 1852–1934) states:

> Electrical signals within a nerve cell flow in **only one direction**: from input (dendrites/soma) to output (axon terminals).

**Flow:**
```
Received at synapses on DENDRITES 
   → Transmitted down the AXON 
   → Passed along at synapses on AXON TERMINALS
```

This directional flow is maintained by the **refractory period** (prevents backward propagation).

> 💡 **Additional Context (from assistant):** Cajal's principle is sometimes called the "law of dynamic polarization." It was a revolutionary insight — he used the Golgi stain method to observe individual neurons and inferred the direction of signal flow from their structure alone, without any electrophysiological recording tools.

## 14. Glossary of Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Action Potential (AP)** | Rapid, self-regenerating electrical signal traveling along the axon; all-or-none |
| **Astrocytes** | Star-shaped glial cells; nourish neurons, regulate environment, maintain BBB |
| **Axon** | Single neuronal projection; transmits APs to synapses |
| **Axon Hillock** | Region where axon originates from soma; trigger zone for AP initiation |
| **Blood-Brain Barrier (BBB)** | Selective barrier maintained by astrocytes; restricts passage of substances from blood to CNS |
| **CNS** | Central Nervous System: brain and spinal cord |
| **Dendrites** | Branch-like extensions of a neuron; receiving zone |
| **Depolarization** | Membrane potential becoming less negative; excitatory |
| **Equilibrium Potential** | Membrane potential where electrical and chemical forces on an ion balance (net flux = 0) |
| **Glial Cells (Neuroglia)** | Non-neuronal cells supporting neurons; 2–10× more numerous than neurons |
| **Hyperpolarization** | Membrane potential becoming more negative; inhibitory |
| **Microglia** | Immune cells of the CNS; phagocytosis and antigen presentation |
| **Myelin** | Fatty insulating substance around axons; enables saltatory conduction |
| **Neurofibrillary Tangles** | Intracellular tau protein aggregates in Alzheimer's disease |
| **Nodes of Ranvier** | Gaps in myelin sheath; sites of AP regeneration in saltatory conduction |
| **Oligodendrocytes** | CNS glial cells; produce myelin sheaths for CNS axons |
| **PNS** | Peripheral Nervous System: all neural tissue outside brain and spinal cord |
| **PSP (Postsynaptic Potential)** | Graded, decremental potential change at postsynaptic membrane |
| **EPSP** | Excitatory PSP; depolarizes membrane toward threshold |
| **IPSP** | Inhibitory PSP; hyperpolarizes membrane away from threshold |
| **Refractory Period** | Post-AP period when another AP is impossible (absolute) or harder (relative) |
| **Resting Membrane Potential** | ~−70 mV; baseline potential maintained by ion asymmetry + Na⁺/K⁺ pump |
| **Saltatory Conduction** | AP "jumping" between Nodes of Ranvier in myelinated axons; fast and efficient |
| **Schwann Cells** | PNS glial cells; produce myelin sheaths for PNS axons |
| **Soma (Cell Body)** | Metabolic center of neuron; contains nucleus and organelles |
| **Spatial Summation** | Summation of PSPs from spatially separate synapses at the same time |
| **Synapse** | Junction between two neurons; transmits chemical or electrical signals |
| **Temporal Summation** | Summation of PSPs from the same synapse at closely spaced time points |
| **Threshold** | −55 mV; depolarization level required to trigger an AP |

## 📝 Review Questions (30 Questions)

---

### Section A: Cells of the Nervous System

**Q1. ⚠️ What are the two main classes of cells in the nervous system? Describe their primary functions.**

**Answer:**
1. **Neurons (nerve cells)**: The signaling units. They receive, process, and transmit information via electrical and chemical signals.
2. **Glial cells (neuroglia)**: Support cells. They outnumber neurons by 2–10×. Functions include structural support, immune defense, nourishment, and modulation of signaling.

---

**Q2. What are the four support functions of glial cells? Give an example of a glial cell type for each.**

**Answer:**
1. **Structural** – All glial cells provide physical scaffolding
2. **Immune** – **Microglia** (phagocytose pathogens/debris, present antigens)
3. **Nourishment** – **Astrocytes** (deliver metabolites, regulate ion/neurotransmitter concentrations, maintain BBB)
4. **Signaling** – **Oligodendrocytes** (CNS) / **Schwann cells** (PNS) (produce myelin for fast AP conduction)

---

**Q3. ⚠️ Describe the role of microglia and explain how they are involved in Alzheimer's disease.**

**Answer:**
Microglia are the **immune cells of the CNS**. They:
- Continuously surveil the brain for damage and infection
- Act as phagocytes to clear pathogens and dead cells
- Present antigens during disease states

In **Alzheimer's disease**, microglia become **hyperactivated**, producing chronic neuroinflammation. This promotes the toxic protein deposits: **amyloid plaques** (extracellular β-amyloid) and **neurofibrillary tangles** (intracellular tau protein).

---

**Q4. What are astrocytes and what are their key functions?**

**Answer:**
Astrocytes are star-shaped glial cells constituting ~50% of brain cells. Key functions:
- **Nourish neurons** (metabolic support via blood vessels)
- **Regulate extracellular ion and neurotransmitter concentrations** (e.g., clearing excess K⁺, glutamate)
- **Maintain the Blood-Brain Barrier** (BBB) — their end-feet wrap around capillaries
- **Modulate synaptic signaling** through bidirectional communication with neurons

---

**Q5. ⚠️ Distinguish between oligodendrocytes and Schwann cells. What disease affects oligodendrocytes?**

**Answer:**

| Feature | Oligodendrocytes | Schwann Cells |
|---|---|---|
| Location | CNS | PNS |
| Coverage | Multiple axon segments | One axon segment |
| Function | CNS myelination | PNS myelination |

**Multiple Sclerosis (MS)** is an autoimmune disease in which the immune system attacks oligodendrocytes, demyelinating CNS axons → slowed/disrupted nerve conduction.

### Section B: Neuron Structure

**Q6. ⚠️ Describe the three components unique to neurons and the functional zone each represents.**

**Answer:**

| Component | Process type | Functional zone |
|---|---|---|
| **Dendrites** | Multiple | Input (receiving) zone |
| **Axon** | Single | Transmitting/conductive zone |
| **Synapses** | Multiple | Output zone |

The dendrites receive inputs; the axon conducts signals; synapses pass signals to the next cell.

---

**Q7. What is arborization, and why is it important for neural function?**

**Answer:**
**Arborization** refers to the number, branching pattern, and disposition of a neuron's projections (primarily dendrites). It determines:
- The neuron's **computational capacity** (how many inputs it can integrate)
- Its **role** in the nervous system
- Its **connectivity** with other neurons and the environment

Neurons with extensive arborization (e.g., Purkinje cells in the cerebellum) can receive thousands of inputs simultaneously.

---

**Q8. How far can an axon extend? Give a biological example.**

**Answer:**
An axon can convey signals over distances ranging from **0.1 mm to 2 m**. 
- Human **motor neuron** axons can exceed **1 meter** (from spinal cord base to the toes)
- Human **sensory neuron** axons can exceed **1.5 meters** (from toes to posterior spinal cord)

This extreme length poses the evolutionary challenge that necessitated action potentials and saltatory conduction.

---

### Section C: Four Signaling Regions

**Q9. ⚠️ Name and describe the four signaling regions of a neuron and the signal type each produces.**

**Answer:**

| Region | Location | Signal Type | Key Property |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Input region** | Dendrites/receptor cells | PSP / receptor potential | Graded; proportional to stimulus |
| **B. Trigger zone** | Axon hillock | Trigger signal | Integrates PSPs; fires if ≥ −55 mV |
| **C. Conductive region** | Axon | Action Potential | All-or-none; non-decremental |
| **D. Output region** | Axon terminals | Synaptic signal | Neurotransmitter release proportional to AP frequency |

---

**Q10. How does the trigger zone translate graded PSP information into binary AP signals?**

**Answer:**
The trigger zone (axon hillock) **sums all PSPs** (spatial + temporal summation). If the summed depolarization reaches threshold (−55 mV), an AP is fired.

Since APs are all-or-none (binary), the **graded amplitude** of the PSP is converted to **AP frequency**: larger/longer PSPs → more APs per unit time. This is the **frequency code**.

---

### Section D: Resting Membrane Potential

**Q11. ⚠️ What is the resting membrane potential? How is it maintained?**

**Answer:**
The **RMP** = approximately **−70 mV** (inside of cell is 70 mV more negative than outside).

Maintained by:
1. **Asymmetric ion distribution**: K⁺ is higher inside; Na⁺, Cl⁻, Ca²⁺ are higher outside
2. **Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase pump**: Actively pumps 3 Na⁺ out for every 2 K⁺ in, maintaining the gradient
3. **Selective membrane permeability**: At rest, membrane is more permeable to K⁺ than Na⁺

---

**Q12. What does depolarization mean and what causes it?**

**Answer:**
**Depolarization** = the membrane potential becomes **less negative** (moves toward 0 mV or beyond). It makes the neuron **more likely to fire an AP**.

Causes:
- Opening of Na⁺ channels → Na⁺ influx (positive charges enter) → inside becomes less negative
- Triggered by: neurotransmitter binding (at dendrites), direct electrical stimulation, or sensory transduction

---

**Q13. What is hyperpolarization and what causes it?**

**Answer:**
**Hyperpolarization** = the membrane potential becomes **more negative** than the RMP. It makes the neuron **less likely to fire**.

Causes:
- K⁺ channels open → K⁺ flows out (positive charges leave) → inside becomes more negative
- Cl⁻ channels open → Cl⁻ flows in (negative charges enter)
- Occurs during: IPSPs, the after-hyperpolarization phase of an AP, and the refractory period

---

### Section E: PSPs and Summation

**Q14. ⚠️ Compare PSPs and Action Potentials across four key properties.**

**Answer:**

| Property | PSP | Action Potential |
|---|---|---|
| Amplitude | **Graded** (proportional to stimulus) | **All-or-none** (constant) |
| Propagation | **Decremental** (decays with distance) | **Non-decremental** (constant amplitude) |
| Distance | Max ~**1 mm** | Can travel **>1 m** |
| Generation | At dendritic synapses | At axon hillock; propagated along axon |

---

**Q15. ⚠️ Explain the difference between spatial and temporal summation.**

**Answer:**
- **Spatial summation**: PSPs from **different spatial locations** (multiple synapses on different dendrites) that arrive at the axon hillock **simultaneously** are summed.
- **Temporal summation**: PSPs from the **same synapse** arriving at **slightly different time points** overlap and sum before fully decaying.

Both types are essential because a single EPSP is insufficient to reach threshold.

---

**Q16. ⚠️ Mathematical problem: RMP = −70 mV. Threshold = −55 mV. Each EPSP = +4 mV, each IPSP = −3 mV. If 8 EPSPs and 4 IPSPs arrive simultaneously, does the neuron fire?**

**Answer:**
$$\Delta V = (8 \times 4) + (4 \times -3) = 32 - 12 = +20 \text{ mV}$$
$$V_{membrane} = -70 + 20 = -50 \text{ mV}$$
$$-50 \text{ mV} > -55 \text{ mV} \Rightarrow \textbf{YES, AP is generated.}$$

---

**Q17. Mathematical problem: RMP = −70 mV. Threshold = −55 mV. Each EPSP = +5 mV. How many EPSPs are minimally needed to fire?**

**Answer:**
$$\Delta V_{needed} = -55 - (-70) = +15 \text{ mV}$$
$$n = \frac{15}{5} = 3 \text{ EPSPs}$$
**Answer: Minimum 3 EPSPs**

---

**Q18. Mathematical problem: Three temporal EPSPs arrive in sequence: +6 mV, +5 mV (slight decay of first), +4 mV (further decay). Starting from −70 mV, threshold = −55 mV. Do they sum to trigger an AP?**

**Answer:**
$$\Delta V = 6 + 5 + 4 = +15 \text{ mV}$$
$$V_{membrane} = -70 + 15 = -55 \text{ mV}$$
At exactly −55 mV, the threshold is **just reached** → **AP is generated** (threshold is the minimum value needed).

### Section F: Action Potentials

**Q19. ⚠️ What is the ionic basis of an action potential? Describe the full sequence.**

**Answer:**
1. **Threshold reached (−55 mV)**: Voltage-gated **Na⁺ channels open**
2. **Depolarization**: Na⁺ rushes in → positive feedback cycle → rapid depolarization to ~+60 mV (Na⁺ equilibrium potential)
3. **Na⁺ channel inactivation**: Na⁺ channels close/inactivate at peak
4. **Repolarization**: Voltage-gated **K⁺ channels open** → K⁺ flows out → membrane repolarizes
5. **Hyperpolarization (undershoot)**: K⁺ channels close slowly → excess K⁺ efflux → membrane overshoots to K⁺ equilibrium potential (more negative than −70 mV)
6. **Return to RMP**: K⁺ channels close → Na⁺/K⁺ pump restores gradients → −70 mV

---

**Q20. ⚠️ What does "conducted without decrement" mean? Why is this important?**

**Answer:**
"Conducted without decrement" means the **amplitude of the AP remains constant** along the entire length of the axon — it does not decay with distance (unlike PSPs).

This is possible because the AP is **actively regenerated** at each successive region of the axon membrane (positive feedback: depolarization opens more Na⁺ channels ahead of the wave).

**Importance:** Enables reliable long-distance communication (e.g., >1 m) without signal loss.

---

**Q21. ⚠️ What is the refractory period? What are its two functional implications?**

**Answer:**
The **refractory period** is the time after an AP during which another AP cannot (absolute) or is difficult to (relative) fire.

**Causes:** Na⁺ channel inactivation + membrane hyperpolarization.

**Functional implications:**
1. **Limits maximum firing frequency** → prevents runaway/overstimulation
2. **Ensures unidirectional AP conduction** → current can only depolarize the membrane *ahead* (the region behind is in refractory state), so APs travel from axon hillock to terminal only

---

**Q22. What is the all-or-none principle of action potentials? What does it imply about information encoding?**

**Answer:**
**All-or-none principle**: APs always have the **same amplitude and duration**, regardless of the size of the triggering stimulus. A barely suprathreshold stimulus produces the same AP as a strongly suprathreshold one.

**Implication**: AP amplitude cannot encode stimulus strength. Instead, information about stimulus intensity is encoded in the **frequency** (firing rate) of APs.

---

### Section G: Frequency Coding

**Q23. ⚠️ How does the neuron communicate the strength of a stimulus?**

**Answer:**
Through **frequency coding** (rate coding):
- Stimulus intensity → PSP amplitude
- Larger PSP → faster it pushes membrane past threshold → shorter inter-AP intervals
- **Higher stimulus intensity** → **higher AP firing rate**
- **Longer stimulus duration** → **longer AP train duration**
- More APs → more neurotransmitter released at output synapse

The graded PSP is thus converted into a temporal frequency code in APs.

---

**Q24. What is the functional difference between a stimulus that has greater amplitude vs. one that has greater duration?**

**Answer:**
- **Greater amplitude stimulus** (same duration): Increases the **frequency** of APs within the given time window → more neurotransmitter released per unit time
- **Greater duration stimulus** (same amplitude): Increases the **duration** of the AP train → more neurotransmitter released over a longer time

Both ultimately increase total neurotransmitter release at the output synapse.

---

### Section H: Saltatory Conduction & Myelin

**Q25. ⚠️ What is saltatory conduction? How does myelin enable it?**

**Answer:**
**Saltatory conduction** is the mechanism by which APs appear to "jump" along myelinated axons from one **Node of Ranvier** to the next.

**Mechanism:**
- Myelin wraps concentrically around the axon, **insulating** it (reducing current leak)
- Ion channels are concentrated **only at Nodes of Ranvier** (bare gaps in myelin)
- APs are regenerated only at nodes → current jumps to the next node
- The AP "jumps" rather than flowing continuously

**Advantages:** Greatly **increases speed** (up to 120 m/s) and **reduces energy cost** (pumps only work at nodes).

---

**Q26. Compare conduction speed in myelinated vs. unmyelinated axons.**

**Answer:**

| Axon Type | Diameter | Conduction Speed |
|---|---|---|
| Large myelinated | ~20 μm | ~120 m/s |
| Small myelinated | ~5 μm | ~20 m/s |
| Unmyelinated | ~1 μm | ~0.5–2 m/s |

Myelination increases speed by 50–100× compared to unmyelinated axons of similar diameter.

---

**Q27. ⚠️ What happens to AP conduction in Multiple Sclerosis? Link to the cellular mechanism.**

**Answer:**
In **Multiple Sclerosis (MS)**:
- The immune system attacks **oligodendrocytes** → demyelination of CNS axons
- Without myelin insulation, saltatory conduction is disrupted
- APs either travel much more slowly (along exposed membrane) or fail to propagate entirely
- Clinical result: motor weakness, sensory loss, visual disturbances, coordination problems

### Section I: Integration & Advanced Questions

**Q28. ⚠️ Explain the full path of information transfer in a neuron, from stimulus to neurotransmitter release.**

**Answer:**
1. **Stimulus** activates receptor/sensory cell → generates **receptor potential** (graded, proportional to stimulus)
2. **PSP** generated at dendrites (EPSP or IPSP) — decremental, graded
3. PSPs travel passively to **axon hillock** (trigger zone)
4. **Spatial and temporal summation** occurs at axon hillock
5. If summation ≥ −55 mV threshold → **Action Potential generated**
6. AP actively propagated (non-decremental) along axon, aided by saltatory conduction in myelinated axons
7. AP reaches **axon terminal** (output region)
8. Frequency of APs determines **amount of neurotransmitter released** at synapse
9. Neurotransmitter binds to receptors on **next neuron** (or muscle/gland) → new PSPs

---

**Q29. Why is a single EPSP insufficient to trigger an AP, and what mechanisms compensate?**

**Answer:**
A single EPSP is insufficient because:
- It is **small in amplitude** (only a few mV)
- It is **decremental** — decays with distance and time
- By the time it reaches the axon hillock, it may be too small to push the membrane past threshold (−55 mV)

**Compensatory mechanisms:**
1. **Spatial summation**: Multiple simultaneous EPSPs from different dendrites sum at the hillock
2. **Temporal summation**: Rapid successive EPSPs from the same synapse overlap before full decay
3. Together, they produce a cumulative depolarization that reaches threshold

---

**Q30. ⚠️ Discuss the significance of the Principle of Dynamic Polarization (Cajal) and explain how it is maintained at the cellular level.**

**Answer:**
The **Principle of Dynamic Polarization** (Santiago Ramón y Cajal) states that signals in a neuron flow in **only one direction**: dendrites → soma → axon → axon terminals.

**Significance:** This ensures organized, predictable information flow within neural circuits, enabling reliable signal processing.

**Cellular maintenance:**
1. **Structural directionality**: Dendrites are specialized for input reception; axon terminals for output
2. **Refractory period**: The region of axon membrane that just fired an AP is temporarily inexcitable — the AP cannot travel backward because the behind-region is in refractory state
3. **Synaptic architecture**: Neurotransmitter release machinery (vesicles, channels) exists only at **presynaptic** terminals (axon side), not on dendrites
